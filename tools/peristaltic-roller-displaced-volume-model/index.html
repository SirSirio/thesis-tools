Peristaltic Roller Occlusion
& Displaced-Volume Model

A two-part geometric reference for the tube-deformation mechanics of a rigid-backed peristaltic pump. Part 1 models the tube cross-section under a roller using the constant-perimeter stadium assumption. Part 2 derives the axial contact length, the volume displaced per roller pass, and the extra arc required for accurate stroke compensation. Each section contains a full derivation, parameter table, and interactive calculator.

Part 1

Tube Cross-Section — The Stadium Model

Purpose & Motivation

When a peristaltic roller presses a flexible tube against a rigid backing, the circular bore does not simply shrink in diameter — it changes shape, flattening into an elongated form before closing entirely. Quantifying that shape matters for two reasons in this design.

First, the volume of fluid a roller displaces depends on the cross-sectional area of the lumen along the contact region, not on any single diameter. Second, the common shorthand of describing occlusion as a percentage is dimensionally ill-posed for a precision dispensing application: occlusion is a local contact phenomenon at the roller, and a percentage implicitly invites the reader to relate it to the whole tube length, which carries no physical meaning. A defensible model must instead report the residual lumen area as a function of how far the roller has closed.

The stadium model provides that. It describes the flattened lumen as a stadium (a rectangle capped by two semicircles, also called a discorectangle) and derives its area from a single physical assumption about the tube wall. The result is a smooth, monotonic area-versus-closure relationship bounded by the open circle at one extreme and complete occlusion at the other.

Physical Assumption — Perimeter Conservation

Elastomeric tube walls (soft PVC in this design) are very nearly incompressible in bulk. Squeezing the tube redistributes wall material rather than compressing its volume. Viewed in cross-section, the inner wall contour retains, to first order, the same arc length as it deforms: the circle of circumference $P = \pi d$ is effectively unrolled into a flatter closed curve of the same total length.

The minimum-bending shape that a fixed-length closed loop naturally adopts when pressed between two parallel flats separated by distance $h$ is a stadium: two straight segments of length $L_w$ joined by two semicircular caps of diameter $h$. The straight runs lie against the roller face and the backing plate; the caps turn the corners with the smallest possible curvature change. This is the entire physical content of the model. Everything that follows is geometry.

Geometry Derivation

Flat width from conserved perimeter. The perimeter of a stadium — two straight segments plus two semicircular caps (which together form one full circle of diameter $h$) — must equal the conserved circular perimeter:

$$P = 2\,L_w + \pi h = \pi d$$

Solving for the flat width:

$$L_w = \dfrac{P - \pi h}{2} = \dfrac{\pi(d - h)}{2}$$

The behaviour at the two limits confirms the model. When $h = d$, then $L_w = 0$: the stadium degenerates back into the original circle. When $h \to 0$, then $L_w \to P/2 = \pi d/2$: the lumen collapses to a flat slit of zero area.

Lumen area. A stadium of straight-width $L_w$ and cap-diameter $h$ has area equal to a central rectangle plus two semicircular caps, which together form one full circle of radius $h/2$:

$$A = L_w \cdot h \;+\; \pi\!\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)^{\!2}$$

Substituting $L_w = \dfrac{\pi(d-h)}{2}$ and expanding:

$$\boxed{A(h) \;=\; \dfrac{\pi}{2}\,d\,h \;-\; \dfrac{\pi}{4}\,h^2}$$

This compact form is the key result. Verification at the two limits: $A(d) = \tfrac{\pi}{2}d^2 - \tfrac{\pi}{4}d^2 = \tfrac{\pi}{4}d^2 = A_0$ (open circle recovered); $A(0) = 0$ (full occlusion). The function is smooth and strictly decreasing in $h$ over $[0,\, d]$, so the residual lumen area is well-defined at every degree of closure. This $A(h)$ is the geometry-based replacement for an occlusion percentage.

The Gap Equation — Squeeze to Residual Area

The printed hardware dimension links directly to the residual lumen via the gap between the roller and the backing wall. A key physical insight: the squeeze is governed by the wall stack, not the bore. Going from untouched to fully occluded:

$$G_{\text{no contact}} \;=\; \mathrm{OD} \;=\; d + 2w$$ $$G_{\text{walls kiss}} \;=\; 2w \qquad \leftarrow \text{inner diameter drops out at occlusion onset}$$ $$G_{\text{compression fit}} \;=\; 2w - \delta$$

At the moment of occlusion the bore has pinched shut, so the inner diameter no longer contributes to the gap — only the two stacked wall thicknesses remain. The interference $\delta$ is the depth of rubber crushed beyond that wall-kiss point. Its sole purpose is to guarantee complete occlusion despite manufacturing and print-tolerance variation. For soft PVC the recommended starting band is:

$$\delta \;=\; (0.10 \;\text{to}\; 0.20) \times 2w$$

Driving $\delta$ beyond approximately $2w$ means crushing wall material rather than merely closing the lumen — accelerating fatigue and risking silent bore drift.

For the interactive figure below, each compression state maps to a residual gap $h$ via a monotonic presentation mapping (not a stress-strain constitutive law):

$$h \;=\; \begin{cases} d & G \geq \mathrm{OD} \\[6pt] \displaystyle d\;\dfrac{G - 2w}{\,\mathrm{OD} - 2w\,} & 2w < G < \mathrm{OD} \\[10pt] 0 & G \leq 2w \end{cases}$$

Assumptions & Limits of Validity

  1. Bulk-incompressible wall. The inner wall conserves arc length as it deforms. Holds well for thin-walled soft elastomers at moderate compression — the foundation of the stadium shape.
  2. Rigid backing. The printed channel plate does not deflect under the roller. Valid for PETG/ASA at these loads; a compliant backing shoe would invalidate the fixed-flats premise.
  3. Idealised stadium. Real flattened PVC develops slight wall thinning and the caps deviate from perfect semicircles under load. Treat $A(h)$ as a clean first-order reference geometry. The empirical correction factor $k$ in Part 2 absorbs this deviation.
  4. Quasi-static, single cross-section. The model describes one axial slice at the roller contact. It does not address tube re-recovery dynamics behind the roller, which is the physical origin of flow pulsation — a separate question requiring flow-trace measurement at Proto 1.

Part 2

Displaced Volume & Axial Contact Length

Purpose

A single roller pass on a rigid-backed peristaltic pump displaces a finite slug of fluid — not at the single contact line but over the axial contact length $L_c$, the length of tube the roller squashes flat as it rolls. This section derives $L_c$, the displaced volume $V_{\text{roller}}$ per pass, and the extra tube arc $\Delta\mathrm{Arc}$ required to compensate for that displacement in a precision dispensing stroke.

The gap equation and stadium cross-section geometry from Part 1 are taken as inputs. In the operating regime $\delta > 0$, occlusion is always complete — the residual lumen area under the roller is zero — so the displacement is entirely determined by the axial contact footprint, not the cross-sectional area at the contact point.

Axial Contact Length $L_c$

A cylinder of radius $R_r$ pressed into a compliant layer over a rigid flat, driven past first contact by radial interference $\delta$, flattens a finite axial length. From the geometry of a circle of radius $R_r$ indented by depth $\delta$, the half-contact half-length is $\ell_{1/2} \approx \sqrt{2\,R_r\,\delta}$, giving the full contact length:

$$\boxed{L_c \;\approx\; k \cdot 2\sqrt{2\,R_r\,\delta}}$$

The factor $k \geq 1$ is an empirical inflation coefficient that absorbs wall thinning and axial bulge omitted by the idealised rigid-Hertz geometry. Published roller-displacement work confirms that perfect-compliance models yield smaller-than-actual displaced volumes Klespitz & Kovács, Sensors & Actuators A, 2022, so $k = 1$ is a strict lower bound. The default $k = 1.15$ is a conservative starting estimate; calibrate from Proto 1 by ink-pressing the flattened footprint and measuring the actual axial length.

A key design consequence follows immediately: a larger roller (16 mm OD, $R_r = 8$ mm) flattens a longer axial section per pass than a smaller roller (10 mm OD, $R_r = 5$ mm), displacing more volume and requiring more arc compensation — but at proportionally lower contact stress and gentler tube wear.

Displaced Volume & Extra Arc

The slug of fluid one roller pinches off over its contact length equals the open lumen area times the contact length (since the lumen is fully closed throughout $L_c$):

$$V_{\text{roller}} \;=\; A_\ell \cdot L_c \;=\; \frac{\pi d^2}{4}\cdot L_c$$

With $N_c$ rollers simultaneously engaged on the contact arc:

$$V_{\text{total}} \;=\; N_c \cdot V_{\text{roller}}$$

To recover that lost volume the stroke must sweep an extra length of tube. Because the tube holds $A_\ell$ of fluid per unit length, the compensating arc per roller is:

$$\Delta\mathrm{Arc} \;=\; \frac{V_{\text{roller}}}{A_\ell} \;=\; L_c \qquad \text{(per engaged roller)}$$ $$\Delta\mathrm{Arc}_{\text{total}} \;=\; N_c \cdot L_c$$

The elegant result is that the compensating arc per roller equals its axial contact length exactly. The lumen area $A_\ell$ cancels in the ratio — the correction is purely geometric and independent of the tube bore. For example: with $R_r = 8$ mm, $\delta = 0.25$ mm, $k = 1.15$, and $N_c = 2$ engaged rollers, $L_c \approx 1.83$ mm and $\Delta\mathrm{Arc}_{\text{total}} \approx 3.66$ mm must be added to the nominal stroke arc.

Important caveat. The constant-perimeter assumption makes $\Delta\mathrm{Arc}$ a lower bound. Real PVC wall thinning and axial bulge increase the actual displaced volume beyond the model prediction. The factor $k$ accounts for this; calibrate $k$ from 20-replicate gravimetry at representative dispense volumes once Proto 1 is assembled: $k = V_{\text{measured}} / V_{\text{model}}$.

Key Assumptions

  1. Rigid backing. The channel plate does not deflect under roller load. Valid for PETG/ASA at these forces.
  2. Constant-perimeter wall. The inner wall conserves arc length and redistributes shape. Provides a lower bound on displaced volume; $k$ corrects upward.
  3. Symmetric bottoming. The roller flattens the tube evenly against the backing with no tilting or rocking.
  4. Quasi-static, no backpressure. Newtonian low-pressure flow assumed. Backpressure modifies the effective interference and should be assessed separately at Proto 1 under representative head conditions.
  5. No tube re-recovery modelling. The model captures displacement by the roller, not the tube's elastic recovery behind it. Pulsation (arising from recovery dynamics) is outside the scope of this geometric model; it requires flow-trace measurement.
