Purpose

The first build meant to actually work to spec. Proto-01 proved the concept and validated the model but carried three known, fixable errors and never occluded the tube without a paper shim. Proto-02:

  • Hit a known, acceptable volume — ideally 5.0 µL/stroke, but a consistent, known volume is equally acceptable: the device spec is ±10 % / not more than ±10 µL over an accumulated dispense, so the binding requirement is low stroke-to-stroke CV, calibrated by step count — not a perfect mean.
  • Fix the three proto-01 errors — Nc, gap, head lock.
  • Make the gap measurable — caliper-access slots read the real installed gap, not the nominal CAD value.
  • Pin down w and k — measure the tube wall before testing; back-calculate the inflation factor from clean post-fix data (proto-01 confounded two error sources).
  • Understand gravimetric vs flow — with a solid build, investigate why the weighed mass reads higher than the integrated flow sensor.

Corrected geometry

\[ L_c = k\cdot 2\sqrt{2R_r\delta} = 3.253\ \text{mm} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta\text{Arc} = N_c\cdot L_c = 2\cdot 3.253 = 6.51\ \text{mm} \]
\[ \text{arc} = \frac{vol}{A} + \Delta\text{Arc} = 24.48 + 6.51 = 30.98\ \text{mm} \quad\Rightarrow\quad R = \frac{N\cdot\text{arc}}{2\pi} = 19.7\ \text{mm} \]

The Nc = 2 correction grows the rotor 17.7 → 19.7 mm. This time the tool's gap prescription G = 2w − δ is carried into CAD (the proto-01 workflow failure was re-estimating it instead).

The gap sweep — the key experiment

The sweep tunes the interference δ = 2w − G — how far the gap squeezes past the measured walls-kiss (2w = 1.82 mm).

Delivery is leak-limited, not arc-limited (key correction from proto-01). The displaced-volume model's arc-compensation term assumes the tube is already fully sealed — it only describes the over-squeezed side. Below the seal threshold the tube backflows, so a looser gap leaks more and delivers less. Delivery vs gap is therefore a hump: rising as you tighten (better seal), peaking at the loosest gap that still fully seals, then slowly declining if over-squeezed. The optimum is the top of the hump — and proto-02's job is to find it.

So the sweep is run firm → loose, hunting the peak:

OrderδTarget GReal expectation
1st (firm)0.301.52 mmfirmly sealed → real delivery; de-risks "does it pump"
2nd0.201.62 mmlooser; if delivery rises, peak is here or looser
3rd0.101.72 mmlikely leaks → delivery drops (≈ proto-01's marginal 1.75)

Read the trend: delivery climbs as you loosen until it doesn't — that turning point is the operating gap. If even 1.52 leaks → go firmer; if 1.72 still climbs → go looser. The fixed rotor (sized at δ = 0.20) makes this a clean single-variable sweep that characterizes both regimes.

Measure the real gap. Each head gets 3 caliper-access slots across the 180° arc (apex + two sides) → records the true installed gap (never nominal) and reveals any clamp-bow / non-uniformity. The first (firm) head doubles as the calibration head for the FDM print offset.

Open questions / risks

  • Seal threshold / leak regime (model limitation) — the arc-compensation model only holds once fully sealed; below the threshold the tube leaks and delivery drops as the gap opens (proto-01 lived here). The model needs a sealing/leak term — a genuine thesis finding.
  • True w — resolved (0.91 mm).
  • Effective k for this tube — provisional 1.15, confirmed only by E5 (sealed side only).
  • Tube wear / hysteresis confounding the sweep if order isn't controlled.
  • Backing-wall bowing under clamp load → non-uniform gap around the arc (E4 detects).
  • System effects deferred — downstream backpressure + tubing dead volume will shift calibration in the assembled device; not tested at pump-only stage.
  • Ambition note — at 5 µL, ±10 % = ±0.5 µL, which a single stroke won't hit open-loop; small commanded volumes rely on stroke-count averaging. State explicitly in the thesis.

Two stacked causes. → Changes for 2.2

Rotor (fix first — it sets the datum)

  • Tighten the bearing pockets — less play pushes 2R back up and lowers σG (a precision fix, not just sizing).
  • PLA-shrink compensation so 2R returns to ~39.4 (R → 19.7), restoring the datum — the existing head radii then stay correct as-designed. Shrink scales with size → scale the model, don't add a fixed offset. The observed 0.36 mm = 0.91 % deficit is an upper bound (part is bearing play): tighten pockets first, then bracket the rotor at +0.4 / +0.6 / +0.8 % XY scale and pick the one landing 2R at 39.4 — or measure a calibration coupon for the factor directly. Don't apply the full 0.91 % and tighten pockets, or you'll overshoot.

Head

  • Centre the wall on the shaft (in-plane) — close the ~0.45 mm too-high (resolve the CAD/seat offset) so the gap is uniform around the arc.
  • Axial alignment (along the shaft) — the rotor sits ~1 mm proud of the head, so the rollers ride to one edge of the channel; register the two axially so the rollers sit centred on the tube. Independent of the in-plane centring.
  • Enlarge the caliper-access holes so the installed wall radius reads directly.

Printer / precision

  • 0.2 mm nozzle (ordered, ~next week) for the bearing pockets — the 0.4 mm nozzle can't hit the 5 mm seat (prints 4.8 = play, or 5.2 = loose), and that play is a direct σG source. Try the finer nozzle + light sanding + PLA-shrink first; metal dowel-pin axles are the fallback if play persists.

Sequence & notes

  • Fix + reprint + re-measure the rotor first, then build and centre the head against the corrected R — the two fixes are naturally sequential, not simultaneous.
  • R 19.52 vs 19.70 slightly lowers swept volume/stroke — fine, volume is calibrated by step count once it seals.

Shrink bars: print at 0.4 mm, not 0.2. Using the MakerWorld Filament Shrinkage Test Bar (two 100 mm bars, X and Y). Thermal shrink is a material property — a percentage of length, essentially nozzle-independent over 100 mm — so 0.2 and 0.4 give the same shrink fraction well inside the ±0.2 % bracket, and 0.4 mm is much faster. The nozzle only matters for fine features (the 5 mm pockets), which the bars don't measure. Keep everything else identical to the rotor print — same spool, same nozzle temp, same bed temp, same plate/orientation.

Shrink procedure

  • Print both bars @ 0.4 mm, matched filament + temps to the rotor print.
  • Let them fully cool before measuring — PLA keeps contracting for minutes after the print ends.
  • Caliper each bar at a few points, average; compute f = (100 − measured) / measured for X and Y separately.
  • If X and Y agree, apply a single XY scale 1 + f; if they differ, per-axis scale.
  • Apply the coupon's material-shrink scale (~0.3–0.5 %) to the rotor — not the full 0.91 % v2.1 deficit (that includes bearing-pocket play, fixed separately), or 2R will overshoot 39.4.

⭐ Key finding — play is ~4.5× the shrink. Only the printed pitch circle shrinks — the bearings are steel. R = pitch radius (14.70 mm, printed) + bearing radius (5.00 mm, steel). So shrink acts on 14.70, not on 39.40: 2 × 14.70 × 0.0022 ≈ 0.065 mm. The v2.1 deficit therefore splits 0.36 mm = shrink ~0.065 (18 %) + bearing play ~0.30 (82 %) — about 0.15 mm of inward nesting per bearing. The measured PLA shrink is at the low end of the 0.2–0.5 % band, so the peg fit is the big lever and shrink comp is a small correction. It also retro-justifies the warning: applying the full 0.91 % would have over-scaled the rotor by ~0.3 mm.

The window is narrow and 5.05 sits at its tight end — so anything that grows the peg risks a rotor that won't assemble.

Rescaling rule — do NOT global-scale the rotor. A global XY scale in the slicer would also scale the peg (5.05 → ~5.06), pushing the empirically-validated fit toward "can't insert". The peg is a fine feature already compensated by the ladder — it needs no shrink scale. Compensate the printed radial geometry only: pitch Ø 29.40 → 29.46 (×1.0022) in CAD, leaving the 10 mm steel bearing and the 5.05 mm peg untouched → 2R = 29.40 + 10 = 39.40 ✓. Scaling the whole rotor instead would compensate the steel too and overshoot 2R by ~0.02 mm.

The rotor took four prints — because the peg is a cone

The v2.2 rotor came out 2R = 39.20, still 0.20 mm short. Two candidate causes demanded opposite fixes — residual play (must be removed mechanically; scaling would bury a CV source) or a dimensional offset (compensable by scale). Guessing wrong would have been worse than doing nothing, so the rotor was calipered without the bearings.

The peg is not a cylinder — it is a truncated cone. The base is fat (press-fit even at 5.05) and the top is loose. With two stacked bearings, the bottom one is located and the top one is free — so the roller does not translate, it pivots about the bottom bearing and tilts inward (~0.6° over the 8 mm stack). Confirmed by measurement: 2R across the top bearing read lower than across the bottom. A tilted roller breaks the line-contact assumption and acts like a screw thread that drives the tube sideways.

A fit ladder cannot fix a cone — every rung is also a cone, so growing the peg only makes the base harder to press while the top stays loose. The fix was to stop using the bad half of it:

 2 bearings (as built)1 bearing (adopted)
Bottom bearinglocated, tight✅ located, tight
Top bearingloose → pivots inward✅ removed
Radial play≈ 0.085 mm (estimate*)✅ none
Skew~0.6°✅ impossible — one bearing cannot pivot

*The 0.085 mm play figure is an estimate, not a direct measurement — derived as bearing bore (5.000 mm) − caliper peg readings (~4.915 mm), and those peg readings were taken on the cone's narrow upper region. The direction and rough size are solid (the top bearing measurably tilted); the third decimal is not.

Play gone → 39.20 became a real number. With two bearings the caliper jaws could have been squeezing the play shut, so 39.20 was ambiguous. With a single bearing there is no play to squeeze — so the remaining 0.90 % pitch shortfall is a fixed, repeatable, compensable dimensional offset. That is what unlocked the print model below.

Why plastic at all? The printed rotor is a deliberate choice, not a compromise discovered too late: it proves the pumping concept fast and cheap, and — just as valuable — it forces learning how to do precision engineering with one specific material and process (FDM PLA), errors included. It is not the endgame: plastic pins print slightly conical, and under the tube's constant inward push plastic also slowly deforms over time (creep) — so a printed peg would not hold its dimension over a long service life. The final pump will be machined in metal, where the pin is a ground steel dowel and neither problem exists. For the proof of concept, plastic with the compensations below is precise enough.

The print model v1 — two errors, not one (superseded)

⚠ Superseded by the ring calibration below. The head printed with this model came out 0.23 mm loose. The ring calibration (⭐ model v2) found why: the symmetric-offset assumption and the transfer of solid-body shrink to bores were both wrong. This section stays as the audit trail of how the thinking evolved.

Why build a model at all? Four prints missed their target, and the obvious loop — print, measure, nudge a number, reprint — is guesswork wearing a lab coat: it converges slowly and teaches nothing transferable. The alternative chosen here was to treat the printer itself as a system to characterize: measure how it transforms CAD dimensions into plastic, capture that transformation as a small model with its own data, and then design against the model — so every compensation parameter in Fusion comes from a measurement, not a hunch.

Ambition check, stated honestly: this model is not claimed to be an accurate or general theory of FDM printing. It is an educated guess with data behind it — just enough model to aim the compensation at the right value on the first try, cheap to recalibrate when the process changes, and falsifiable by the very next print. That is all it needs to be.

The core realization: a 3D print does not have one dimensional error, it has two — and they behave completely differently depending on what kind of dimension you are measuring.

Calibration — one part gave both constants

The rotor happened to carry a steel-referenced span (2R, across the bearings) and a plastic surface (the flange OD). That is exactly the pair the model needs — so a single print calibrated it completely.

✅ The scale is validated. The rescaled rotor was predicted to hit 2R = 39.40 and measured 39.42 / 39.38 → mean 39.40, error 0.00. R = 19.70 mm — the design value, recovered exactly. The 0.04 mm out-of-round was also predicted (X/Y shrink anisotropy under a single mean scale) and costs ~1.4 % worst case — periodic, not random, so it averages over a stroke.
The coupon design was flawed — and that is the real lesson. A single 100 mm bar is measured between two convex faces, so it reports 100·s + 2·offset — one equation, two unknowns. It cannot separate scale from offset. The apparent "0.22 % vs 0.90 % discrepancy" that sent us hunting for a phantom nozzle-shrink term was an artifact of the measurement, not a physical effect. The correct coupon: print two bars of different lengths at the same nozzle (e.g. 100 mm and 20 mm) → two equations, two unknowns → s and offset independently, from one print.

Process lock. k and SurfaceOffset are calibrated for a process, not a material — valid only for the exact slicer profile that produced the rotor. Layer height, wall count, flow, speed, temperature, nozzle: change any one and the calibration is void. Adaptive layer height is off — it cannot help (the gap-critical arc is a vertical wall, so it has no stair-stepping at any layer height) and it would make SurfaceOffset Z-dependent, varying the gap along the roller's 4 mm contact band.

What the FIRM head actually tests. The scale k is validated. The offset is fitted on a convex surface and assumed equal-and-opposite on concave ones — the standard assumption, but slicers ship separate hole and contour compensation for a reason. So: print the FIRM head alone, then caliper the installed gap through the access slots (top + both sides). If it reads 1.52, the print model is validated in both directions and MID/LOOSE go straight to print. If it reads tighter, the concave offset is larger and earns its own constant. One head to learn the number, or three heads to learn it three times.

⭐ Printer calibration — ring artifact → two-line compensation (model v2)

The goal is a calibration, not a physics model. After the head printed 0.23 mm loose — the third oversized part in a row — the guess-and-reprint loop was replaced by the standard approach: fit the printer's transfer function printed = f(CAD) on a test artifact, then invert it to answer the only question that matters: what CAD value gives the printed value I want? This is textbook linear calibration / inverse prediction [7], and purpose-built test artifacts for AM systems are standardized practice (ISO/ASTM 52902 [6], per-feature CAD compensation [5]). The ring coupon is a 52902-style artifact trimmed down to the two feature classes the pump actually uses. The method transfers to any printer — only the two fitted lines are specific to this machine, nozzle, filament and profile.

Measurement: digital caliper, two perpendicular diameters per feature, averaged (a circle's diameter is direction-independent, so ring orientation doesn't matter). Bores with the knife edges, ODs with the flat jaws. The mid ring was printed in triplicate to measure print-to-print repeatability.

Noise — two stacked sources, stated honestly. (1) Printer repeatability, captured by the triplicate ring. (2) Measurement error, including deformation: the rings are thin plastic — measuring a bore pushes the ring outward (tends to over-read), measuring an OD squeezes it (tends to under-read), worst on the largest ring. Care was taken, but this can't be fully eliminated — a concrete example of why a measurement is never exact. The random part is absorbed into σ below; any small systematic part folds into the fitted intercepts, which is acceptable for a calibration, since future parts are measured the same way.

The data, plotted — one law per feature class

The analysis, step by step — nothing more than the data supports

  • 1 · Split by feature class before fitting — internal and external features are known to behave differently ([2], [3], and every slicer's separate hole-compensation setting).
  • 2 · Plot first — the points fall on straight lines in both classes, so a two-parameter line is enough; no curvature term is justified.
  • 3 · Least-squares line per class — two parameters because there are two physical mechanisms: an error that grows with size (shrink → slope) and one that doesn't (bead geometry → intercept).
  • 4 · Judge by residuals — a residual is measured − predicted for each point: the leftover error, in mm. Residual SD: 0.023 mm (outer) · 0.040 mm (inner) — the typical miss of each line.
  • 5 · Compare against repeatability — the triplicate gives σ ≈ 0.03–0.05 mm. Residuals ≈ σ → the lines capture everything measurable. Stop modeling.
  • 6 · One hypothesis test — are the two slopes different? −0.0065 ± 0.0005 vs +0.0016 ± 0.0009: they differ by ≈ 8 standard errors. Per-class compensation isn't a preference; the data forces it.
Why R² is reported but is NOT the headline number. R² measures how much of the variation in the data the line explains compared to just using the average. The outer deviations swing −0.11 → −0.50 (large variation, mostly slope) → R² = 0.98. The inner class has a true slope of ≈ 0, so its deviations barely vary — what remains is mostly print noise, and no line can "explain" noise → R² = 0.54, even though the inner fit predicts just as accurately (residual SD 0.040 mm). When the correct answer is a flat line, R² is low by construction. A thermostat holding a room at 21 °C: the model "always 21.0" has R² ≈ 0 — and is accurate to a tenth of a degree. The honest accuracy metric is the residual SD against the repeatability σ.

Post-mortem of model v1 — what the ±0.11 actually did. v1 measured +0.11 (proud) on the convex flange and assumed the mirror image, −0.11 intrusion, for the concave arc — then applied the solid-body scale (×1.009) to everything. Fix 1 — the gap: apply model v2, retire the old compensation

The v2.2 head printed 0.23 mm loose because model v1 applied a solid-body scale (×k) to a concave arc — bores barely scale. Model v2 replaces k and SurfaceOffset on the head with the inner rule (CAD = (target + 0.208)/1.00164). Both head radii drop ~0.23 mm — exactly the phantom shrink removed.

Where k lives now. The scale k = 1.00906 survives only on the rotor pitch — a centre-to-centre span, where it was validated to 2R = 39.40. It no longer touches the head: a concave arc is a different error regime, and conflating the two was the whole v1 mistake. SurfaceOffset is retired from the head (folded into the rule's intercept).

Fix 2 — the apex: the "unexplained 0.37 mm" was a CAD offset

The head's arc must be centred on the shaft. v2.1's apex sat ~0.45 mm too high (0.08 mm dovetail stop + ~0.37 mm unexplained). A direct measurement closed it: from the shaft edges (23.58 & 28.51 → centre 26.05, and the 4.93 mm span confirms the 5 mm shaft), the real holder's dovetail → shaft is 26.05 mm, but the head CAD assumed 25.70. So the head built its arc centre 0.35 mm above the real shaft → the apex opens by ~0.35, the sides barely move (a vertical shift is second-order there). That is the 0.37 mm.

The lever is the seat, not the dovetail width. A dovetail is a slide — its trapezoid registers the head laterally (play is already ~zero — leave it) and how deep it seats is set by the hard stop. So the fix lowers the seat by 0.35 mm (SeatDrop = HolderDovetailToShaft − DovetailToArcCenter), dropping the arc centre onto the shaft — not shrinking the dovetail. Parameterised off the measured HolderDovetailToShaft (like RotorFlangeDMeasured), so re-measuring the holder is a one-number change. A residual ~0.08 mm (the stop printing high) is left to measure on the print through the slots — not chased blind.

Corner relief — a seating tweak, not a fit change

FDM rounds the female slot's internal corners, so a sharp male corner rides on point contact and props the head up — the "press harder and it shifts a bit" wobble. The dovetail fits are already nominal (male 18.97 / 19, female 27.95–28.08 / 28, lateral play ~zero), so this isn't a tolerance change: just relieve the male bottom tips so the flat faces seat, and keep the flat hard-stop face intact — it's the trustworthy vertical datum. Goal is to kill the force-dependence, not accommodate it with flex.

What the campaign established

  • The −8.9 % deficit is geometric, not dynamic — speed moves the dose only ~2 %, so the shortfall lives in gap/occlusion. Feeds the displaced-volume model's effective k (E5). Correcting it is a step-count multiplier, not a redesign.

Honest scope — what this does NOT prove. Single build, single tube, water only, no backpressure. Single-5 µL-dose precision is bounded (≤ ~6 %), not measured — the √N estimate is inflated by run-to-run drift, and a 0.1 mg balance cannot resolve it directly; a finer balance or the ISO photometric route is the follow-up. The thesis claim is framed accordingly: as a metered multi-stroke pump, v2.3 is accurate, pipette-class precise, and optimal at its design speed; as a single-dose device the accuracy holds but per-dose precision is bounded, not proven.

Does a 12 V test say anything about the 24 V design? — for this question, yes

The test ran at 12 V because that is the bench supply available; the device is designed for 24 V. Whether that invalidates the result depends entirely on which property is being measured, so the reasoning is written out rather than asserted.

  • What the driver actually does. A DRV8825 does not connect the supply straight to the motor. It is a current regulator: a small potentiometer on the board sets a target coil current, and the chip switches the supply on and off tens of thousands of times a second to hold the coils at that current. A stepper's torque comes from the current in its coils, not from the supply voltage — same current setting, same torque, whether the board is fed 12 V or 24 V.
  • So what is the supply voltage for? Speed. A coil resists sudden changes in current (it is an inductor), and a spinning motor generates a voltage of its own that opposes the driver, growing with speed. Both make it harder to reach the current target inside the short time each step allows. A higher supply voltage is the headroom the driver uses to win that race: it buys torque at high speed, and does not raise the torque available at standstill.
  • Why 12 V is enough here. At the instant a motor starts from rest, speed is zero, so there is no opposing voltage and the coil has as long as it needs to reach the set current. The torque available to break away from standstill is set by the current limit alone, and is identical at 12 V and 24 V.
Both observations sit in exactly that regime. The JLB failed to start — the standstill case — so it would fail identically at 24 V; the 12 V supply is not the reason it failed. The Creality started and ran, and moving to 24 V can only give the driver more headroom, never less — so a pass at 12 V is a conservative pass.
What was not measured — recorded so none of it is later mistaken for a result. (1) ⭐ The driver current setting was not measured — now the highest-value open item, for the reason above. It was left at the value used for the standard NEMA 17 rather than re-tuned per motor. The baseline Wantai is rated 2.5 A and the JLB 1.3 A, so if the setting was left at the Wantai's value the JLB was driven at or above its rating and the failure is real — but that is inferred from an assumed setting, not measured. One multimeter reading closes it. (2) Nothing was weighed or purchased — every weight and price above is a catalogue or vendor figure, not a scale reading or a checkout total. Both tested motors are in hand, so a kitchen scale settles the weights immediately. (3) The Creality's margin is unknown — it turns the head; by how much it exceeds the requirement was not measured, so the bracket has a hard floor but a soft ceiling. (4) The JK42HS40-1704 is untested — it is recommended on published-spec equivalence to the Creality, which is equivalence on paper, not on the bench. (5) High-speed behaviour at 24 V is untested, since that is the one regime where supply voltage does matter. (6) One head, one tube, water, no backpressure — the torque demand measured here belongs to the 1.52 mm gap; a firmer gap demands more, and a downstream needle adds more still.
