  • Three ways to move a few microlitres: push a plunger down a bore, flex a diaphragm between two check valves, or squeeze a tube.
  • In the first two the machine itself is wetted — plunger, barrel, diaphragm, valve leaflets all hold the reagent.

  • Peristalsis closes the wetted boundary on the tube alone, and the tube is a consumable.

The dispenser assembles the reaction; the reader is what turns it into a number.

Fluorescence · excite blue, read the green that comes back RT-LAMP · RPA · qPCR · CRISPR

Best sensitivity per euro, and PANPOC’s own assay already works this way. The heater sets the battery, not the optics.

Colorimetric · the pH drops and the mix turns pink to yellow LAMP · gold-bead immunoassays

The cheapest reader that works — but a crude sample can suppress the colour change and read as a false negative.

Turbidimetry · the reaction clouds the tube as it runs LAMP only

Weak, and it reads LAMP and nothing else. It is on the list because the parts are the ones colorimetry already needs.

Lateral flow · read the test line against the control line immunoassay strips

No heater at all, the lowest power draw. Its hard part is registering the strip, not the optics — what the alignment module already does.

Electrochemical · binding becomes a current at an electrode enzyme and binding assays

No optics at all, so light, turbidity and colour stop mattering — arguably the best fit for what this dispenser hands over.

Chemiluminescence and ECL · the label emits its own light immunoassay labels

The borderline case: no lamp to power, but it wants a photomultiplier and a genuinely light-tight chamber.

  • Frame first: the dispenser assembles the reaction, the reader does the signal transduction. Five modalities are reachable inside a portable module, a sixth is borderline, and everything else — magnetic GMR, surface plasmon resonance, Raman, nanopore — needs custom silicon or lab-grade optics and is out.
  • 1 · fluorescence — a dye intercalates or a probe is unquenched as amplicon accumulates; excite blue, read the longer-wavelength emission, and the Stokes shift lets a filter pull a weak signal off a strong source. Real-time, so it gives a time-to-positive. This is what PANPOC itself uses: probe-based multiplex fluorescent LAMP, developed at DTU. Commercially it is GeneXpert, cobas liat, and Abbott ID NOW. The battery argument is that the heater block, not the optics, dominates the power budget.
  • 2 · colorimetric — LAMP in a weakly buffered mix drops the pH and phenol red turns yellow; visible by eye, quantified as a ratio of two LED channels. Gold nanoparticles are the immunoassay version, red dispersed and blue aggregated. Cheapest possible reader, and its weakness is this thesis’s own argument in miniature: a crude, high-buffer sample suppresses the colour change and reads as a false negative, so it is only as good as the preparation.
  • 3 · turbidimetry — LAMP’s pyrophosphate precipitates with magnesium and clouds the tube (Mori 2001). Eiken sells it as the Loopamp Realtime Turbidimeter. Same LED-and-photodiode hardware as colorimetry, but LAMP-only and the weakest signal of the set.
  • 4 · lateral flow — wicked strip, labelled antibody captured at a test line, reflectance measured against the control line. White LED and a camera, no heater, so the lowest power of the six. The hard part is mechanical registration of the strip rather than the optics, which is exactly the competence the alignment module demonstrates. Good line if asked what is adjacent to this work.
  • 5 · electrochemical — no light path at all. Hold an electrode at a fixed potential and measure the current from a redox mediator, the glucose-meter lineage, or watch charge-transfer resistance rise as mass binds. A potentiostat is one IC now. Immune to ambient light, turbidity and colour, so a bead-laden crude lysate does not matter. Costs: strip-to-strip variation needs a calibration code, electrodes foul, and electron transfer is temperature-dependent. The blood-glucose meter is the proof that this class scales to a battery-powered, untrained-user product.
  • 6 · chemiluminescence and ECL — the label emits its own light, so there is no lamp; ECL switches it on electrically, which is how Roche’s Elecsys platform works. But a photomultiplier or SiPM and a genuinely light-tight chamber are more box than a portable module has, so it sits on the edge rather than inside the set.
  • If asked why not just use a phone: a phone gives a camera and compute for free, but no control of exposure geometry, no filters and no heater. It covers lateral flow and colorimetry, nothing below them.
  • If asked what the dispenser buys the reader: every one of these assumes the reaction mix was assembled correctly. The reader is the cheap half; preparation is the hard half. That is this thesis’s argument restated from the detection side.

  • Two things can go wrong with a dose, and they are independent. Accuracy is the systematic offset from the true volume; precision is the random scatter between repeats. The thesis reports both, so both have to be on the table.
  • Middle board: the average lands on the true volume, but no two doses match — random error, which is what the dashed ring measures. Right board: every dose matches the last, and all of them are wrong by the same amount — systematic error, which is what the arrow measures, from the true centre to the mean.
  • Underneath: the protocol asks for 5 µL to 1000 µL inside one procedure. Dividing one by the other is a two-hundred-fold span — that is my own arithmetic on the protocol volumes, not a published figure. A single air-displacement channel holds its precision across only about one order of magnitude, so covering that span by hand means exchanging tips or whole tools part-way through.
  • And it is not a question of skill: across 20 laboratory professionals, dispensing scatter rose from 0.2 % at 1 mL to 5.7 % at 10 µL, and years of experience made no difference (Lippi et al. 2017). The requirement I set for the machine is ±10 % of target, with maximum absolute deviation no more than 10 µL.

  • 1 · the plate — PANPOC is the portable instrument PAIR is building: influenza, SARS-CoV-2, anything with pandemic potential, meant for sites such as airports and border controls. That is the hall we started in. Before it can read a swab, a bench has to turn that swab into purified RNA. This is the thesis figure redrawn — one tube, seven stages, and the legend for what is floating in it.
  • 2 · release and lyse — the swab is agitated in guanidine thiocyanate. That breaks the cells open and stops the enzymes that would otherwise destroy the RNA. A volume goes in, and the tube now holds the sample and everything else that came off the swab.
  • 3 · add the control — a known quantity of synthetic RNA, the light blue one. It is there so a negative result can be told apart from a failed extraction: if the control does not come through, the run failed.
  • 4 · bind — ethanol, and silica-coated magnetic beads. The RNA sticks to the bead surface; everything else stays loose in the liquid.
  • 5 · capture and discard — a magnet pins the beads to the tube wall and the liquid is pipetted away, carrying everything that did not bind. That magnet is what removes the centrifuge, and it is the reason this protocol can leave a laboratory at all.
  • 6 · wash, twice — 70 % ethanol in, captured again, drawn off again. Twice.
  • 7 · dry — residual ethanol evaporates. Nothing added, nothing taken away; the one stage that only waits.
  • 8 · elute — the magnet comes off, water is added, the RNA releases back into solution. That is what finally goes to the detector.
  • 9 · the close — now look at the right-hand column. Four of those seven stages are a measured volume going in, and a fifth adds as well as removes. A general device for sample preparation is a device that places measured volumes; that is the whole job. Detection can travel to the gate; the upstream preparation cannot, because every volume in that preparation is placed by hand, at a bench.
